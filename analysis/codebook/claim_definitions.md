# Claim Definitions Codebook

**Project:** Preprint-to-Publication Claim Change Study
**Version:** 0.1 (draft for review)
**Date:** 2026-04-22
**Maintainer:** Ruslan Rust, USC

## 1. Purpose

This document defines, operationally and strictly, what counts as a *primary claim* and a *secondary claim* in a scientific abstract. It is the single source of truth for:

1. Human annotators validating a reference set.
2. The LLM extraction prompt (which will quote these definitions verbatim).
3. The preregistration and the Methods section of the manuscript.

Any change to these definitions after lock requires a version bump and a re-annotation of the validation set.

## 2. What is an abstract claim?

An **abstract claim** is a declarative statement in the abstract that asserts a finding, relationship, mechanism, effect, or interpretation produced by the present study.

A claim must satisfy ALL of the following:

1. **Assertive.** It states something the authors assert to be true based on their data, not something they plan to do or something already known.
2. **Study-own.** It is generated by the work presented, not attributed to prior literature.
3. **Declarative and verifiable.** It has a truth value that could in principle be confirmed or refuted.
4. **Located in Results or Conclusions function.** It carries the semantic function of a result, inference, or interpretation, regardless of sentence position.

### 2.1 Not a claim (exclusion list)

The following are explicitly excluded:

- **Background statements** about prior knowledge, gaps, or motivation.
  *Example: "Alzheimer's disease is the most common form of dementia."*
- **Aim or intent statements.**
  *Example: "Here we sought to determine whether X regulates Y."*
- **Method descriptions.**
  *Example: "We used single-cell RNA-seq in 12 wild-type mice."*
- **Pure tool or resource announcements** without an empirical finding attached.
  *Example: "We present scClaim, a Python package for claim extraction."*
  (If a benchmark result is reported, that benchmark is a claim.)
- **Definitional sentences** defining terms or concepts.
- **Future-directed statements.**
  *Example: "Future work should test this in humans."*
- **Generic significance filler.**
  *Example: "These findings have broad implications for the field."*
  (Only counts if the implication is specific and testable.)

## 3. Primary claim

### 3.1 Definition

The **primary claim** is the single statement that most directly answers the study's central question and that the authors foreground as the main take-home message of the paper.

### 3.2 Strict criteria

A candidate statement is the primary claim if and only if it satisfies ALL of:

1. It corresponds to the study's main stated or implied aim.
2. Removing it would leave the abstract without a clear take-home message.
3. Its scope covers the study as a whole, not only a sub-analysis.
4. It is the claim that the title, if read with the abstract, is most plausibly designed to advertise.

### 3.3 Disambiguation heuristics (apply in order)

Use these only when criteria 3.2 do not yield a unique winner:

1. **Position heuristic.** Prefer the statement in the final one to three sentences of the abstract (the "Conclusions" slot in IMRaD abstracts, or the last results sentence in unstructured abstracts).
2. **Cue-phrase heuristic.** Prefer statements introduced by: *we show, we demonstrate, we find, we report, these results establish, we conclude, collectively, together these data*.
3. **Title alignment heuristic.** Prefer the statement whose content noun phrases overlap most with the title.
4. **Scope heuristic.** Prefer the statement with the broadest, study-level scope over narrower sub-findings.

Exactly **one** primary claim is extracted per abstract. If two statements tie under all four heuristics, merge them into a single compound primary claim using "and".

### 3.4 Boundary notes

- The primary claim is not necessarily the most quantitatively impressive finding. It is the claim the authors elevate.
- A methods paper's primary claim is typically a performance claim (e.g. "method X outperforms Y on benchmark Z"), not the existence of the method itself.
- In a purely descriptive atlas paper, the primary claim is the most general descriptive generalization drawn from the atlas, not a list of cell types.

## 4. Secondary claims

### 4.1 Definition

A **secondary claim** is any additional assertive, study-own, declarative finding or interpretation in the abstract that is distinct from the primary claim.

### 4.2 Strict criteria

A statement is a secondary claim if and only if it satisfies ALL of:

1. Meets all four conditions in Section 2 (is a claim).
2. Is not the primary claim under Section 3.
3. Is **semantically distinct** from the primary claim. A paraphrase, hedged restatement, or generalization of the primary claim does not count as a new secondary claim.
4. Is **semantically distinct** from any higher-ranked secondary claim.

### 4.3 Ranking of secondary claims

Rank secondary claims 1 (most important) through k. When two secondary claims tie, apply, in order:

1. Claims that provide mechanism for the primary claim outrank those that do not.
2. Claims that define the scope, boundary, or moderator of the primary claim outrank applied-implication claims.
3. Within the same tier, preserve order of appearance in the abstract.

For this study we extract **exactly the top two secondary claims (S1, S2)**. If fewer than two qualifying secondary claims exist, record `NA` for the missing slots with a reason code (see Section 7).

### 4.4 Typology (tag each secondary claim)

Each secondary claim is tagged with exactly one of:

- `mechanism` - proposes a mechanism or mediator for the primary finding.
- `moderator` - specifies a condition, subgroup, or boundary where the primary effect differs.
- `secondary_outcome` - a separate outcome tested alongside the primary outcome.
- `quantitative_detail` - an effect size, rate, or benchmark quantifying a finding.
- `applied_implication` - a specific, testable translational or practical implication.
- `negative_result` - an explicit null or absence finding the authors report as informative.

## 5. Structured claim attributes

Each extracted claim (primary or secondary) is represented as a structured object:

| Field | Type | Description |
|---|---|---|
| `text_verbatim` | string | The exact sentence(s) from the abstract, quoted. |
| `text_normalized` | string | Minimal paraphrase: subject, verb, object, qualifiers. |
| `subject` | string | The entity or variable being asserted about. |
| `predicate` | string | The relation asserted (e.g. increases, is required for, correlates with). |
| `object` | string | The target entity, variable, or outcome. |
| `direction` | enum | `positive`, `negative`, `null`, `non-directional`. |
| `effect_type` | enum | `causal`, `associative`, `descriptive`, `predictive`, `performance`. |
| `quantifier` | string or NA | Explicit numerical effect if given (e.g. "2.4-fold", "p<0.001", "AUC=0.91"). |
| `hedging_level` | enum | `strong` (no hedge), `moderate` (may, suggests, consistent with), `weak` (could, might, potentially). |
| `scope` | enum | `universal`, `population`, `subgroup`, `single-case`. |
| `novelty_claim` | bool | Whether authors frame this as novel (first, novel, previously unknown). |

All claims must be instantiable as this object. If any required field cannot be filled, record `NA` and log the reason in `parse_notes`.

## 6. Claim change categories (for preprint-vs-published comparison)

Each (preprint claim, published claim) pair is assigned exactly one **primary label** from a three-level scheme. Pairs labelled `minor` also receive a **hedging sub-label**. Pairs labelled `major` receive **exploratory tags** that are descriptive only.

### 6.1 Primary label (three mutually exclusive levels)

| Label | Rule |
|---|---|
| `unchanged` | Same assertion. Wording identical or a trivial paraphrase with no change in direction, scope, magnitude, effect type, or hedging. |
| `minor` | The assertion, direction, scope, magnitude, and effect type are all preserved. Only the **hedging level differs**. |
| `major` | At least one of the following holds: the direction of effect flipped; the scope broadened or narrowed; the numerical magnitude changed beyond rounding; the effect type changed (e.g. causal to associative); the claim was replaced by a distinct claim; the claim was added in the published version; the claim was removed in the published version. |

Decision rule: apply in order. If any `major` condition holds, the pair is `major`, regardless of hedging. If not `major` and hedging differs, the pair is `minor`. Otherwise `unchanged`.

### 6.2 Hedging sub-label (only when primary label is `minor`)

| Sub-label | Rule |
|---|---|
| `strengthened` | Hedge removed or verb moved toward greater certainty (e.g. *may regulate* -> *regulates*; *suggests* -> *demonstrates*). |
| `weakened` | Hedge added or verb moved toward lower certainty (e.g. *is required for* -> *may contribute to*). |

Hedging lexicon for mapping verbs and modals to certainty tiers will be maintained in Appendix A (to be added).

### 6.3 Exploratory tags (only when primary label is `major`, not mutually exclusive)

These tags are **descriptive only**, not rater primary decisions. They are assigned after the primary label and may co-occur on the same pair. No kappa target is set for these tags.

- `direction_flipped` - sign of effect reversed.
- `scope_changed` - generality broadened or narrowed (e.g. mouse-only to mammalian).
- `magnitude_changed` - numerical effect size differs beyond rounding.
- `effect_type_changed` - e.g. causal framing shifted to associative.
- `replaced` - preprint claim dropped, a distinct claim occupies the functional slot.
- `added` - claim present in published abstract only (applies to secondary slots).
- `removed` - claim present in preprint only (applies to secondary slots).

### 6.4 Kappa targets (for lock, see Section 9)

- Primary label (3-level): Cohen's kappa >= 0.75.
- Hedging sub-label (2-level, conditional on `minor`): Cohen's kappa >= 0.70.
- Exploratory tags: no kappa target; reported as agreement percentages in the supplement.

## 7. Edge cases and decision rules

1. **Unstructured abstract with no Conclusions sentence.** Use Section 3.3 heuristics in order.
2. **Abstract with multiple studies or two datasets.** Treat as a single paper. The primary claim is the statement the authors present as the integrated conclusion across datasets.
3. **Review or perspective articles.** Out of scope for this study. Exclude upstream using publication type filters.
4. **Meta-analyses.** In scope. The primary claim is the pooled estimate's interpretation.
5. **Abstracts with only a primary claim and no qualifying secondary claims.** Record S1 and S2 as `NA` with reason `no_qualifying_secondary`.
6. **Abstracts with >2 qualifying secondary claims.** Rank per Section 4.3 and keep top two. Archive the full list in `secondary_candidates_all`.
7. **Ambiguous sentences that mix a claim with background.** Split the sentence and extract only the claim portion into `text_normalized`, while keeping the full sentence in `text_verbatim`.
8. **Hedged claim later restated strongly, or vice versa, in the same abstract.** Treat as one claim; record the stronger wording in `text_verbatim` and note the hedging asymmetry in `parse_notes`.

### Reason codes for missing claims

- `no_qualifying_secondary` - fewer than two secondary claims met Section 4.2.
- `abstract_unparseable` - formatting or language issue.
- `non_empirical_abstract` - no empirical finding present (should be excluded upstream).
- `translation_error` - non-English abstract that did not translate cleanly.

## 8. Worked examples

### Example A - structured neuroscience abstract

> Background. Microglia regulate synapse pruning during development. Methods. We generated Trem2-conditional KO mice and performed two-photon imaging in V1. Results. Trem2 deletion reduced spine elimination by 38% (p<0.001). Loss was restricted to layer 2/3 pyramidal neurons. Spontaneous firing rates were unchanged. Conclusions. Trem2 is required for developmental pruning of layer 2/3 cortical synapses.

- **Primary claim.** Trem2 is required for developmental pruning of layer 2/3 cortical synapses. (Section 3.3 position + cue-phrase.)
- **Secondary claim 1 (`moderator`).** The pruning defect is restricted to layer 2/3 pyramidal neurons.
- **Secondary claim 2 (`negative_result`).** Trem2 deletion does not alter spontaneous firing rates.
- **Not claims.** "Microglia regulate synapse pruning during development" (background). "We generated Trem2-conditional KO mice" (methods).

### Example B - unstructured methods abstract

> Single-cell RNA-seq pipelines are limited by doublet contamination. We present DoubletX, a transformer-based doublet detector. Across 11 public datasets, DoubletX improved AUPRC by 14% over the best existing method. Gains were largest in high-multiplexing protocols. DoubletX runs in under five minutes on a laptop.

- **Primary claim.** DoubletX improves doublet detection AUPRC by 14% over the best existing method across 11 datasets. (Performance claim, not the tool announcement.)
- **Secondary claim 1 (`moderator`).** Improvements are largest in high-multiplexing protocols.
- **Secondary claim 2 (`quantitative_detail`).** DoubletX runs in under five minutes on a laptop.
- **Not a claim.** "We present DoubletX" (tool announcement without finding).
